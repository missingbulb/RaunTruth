# Detection, not reading, decides bookshelf readers

Twenty years of book-spine work, from Stanford's 2010 smartphone inventory to a 2024 CLIP-matching study, frame the problem the way our Bookshelf Reader does: segment each spine, normalise its orientation, find text lines, recognise them, then match against a list. The one recent end-to-end measurement puts most of the loss at **segmentation, not reading or matching**. Matching accuracy falls from **0.918 with ground-truth spine regions to 0.641 with automatic detection** against a 15k-title list ([Llabrés et al. 2024, arXiv preprint, DAS 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19812)). Our classical pipeline (shelf rows from long horizontal edges, near-vertical lines inside each row, a text map that vetoes lines through lettering) sits in the same family as published classical systems. The one classical segmenter with a reported precision and recall, Talker's, scores P 80.7 / R 87.3 and P 70.4 / R 72.6 on its two sets, tuned on the same images, at a stricter match (IoU ≥ 0.67) on ground truth without border-cut spines — not comparable to ours, and not clearly above it. What the literature does offer is a set of transferable, CPU-cheap upgrades. Rotate by the dominant book direction first. Generate too many candidate spines and choose a non-overlapping set globally. Merge candidates by clustering instead of greedy suppression. Check each box against its row neighbours and then recover gaps. Read per line rather than per crop. Pass several orientation and OCR hypotheses to the matcher instead of committing to one. It also offers one realistic learned step, a nano oriented-box YOLO (about 11 MB), measured on a CC0 library dataset we can download. There is **no Hebrew spine data, no home-shelf benchmark and no published spine accuracy for Tesseract**, so our own 133 labelled books remain the only honest score of what we care about. Our P 0.67 / R 0.89 on 6 photos is indicative only: one owner, a few shelves, and a sample too small to separate a 5-point change from noise.

## Everyone built segment, read, match, and segmentation stayed the wall

The field runs in three waves. Classical pipelines (2003–2015) used finite-state model fitting, Hough lines, active contours and MSER text cues, almost always on library shelves. Examples are Taira 2003, Chen et al.'s ACM MM 2010 inventory app, Tsai et al.'s 2011 image-plus-text hybrid, and Talker & Moses at WACV 2014. A 2016–2017 IEEE paper on multi-row shelves uses **exactly our skeleton**: horizontal edges split the rows, then vertical lines split the spines ([IEEE Xplore 7890886, peer-reviewed, abstract only](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7890886/)). The CNN-assisted wave (2016–2022) came next. Penn State's Smart Library is its clearest member, and it **demoted Hough to estimating the dominant book direction only**, because low-level line segmentation "can be easily influenced by lighting conditions or low contrast between books". It rotated the image by that direction and found the boundaries between books by non-max suppression on a text/non-text CNN saliency map. The authors claim this separates neighbours "even when two neighbor book spines have the same color", but they give no segmentation accuracy at all ([Yang et al. 2016, arXiv preprint](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1611.07385)). The current wave (2021–2025) uses oriented detectors and foundation models, such as Oriented R-CNN, YOLOv8-OBB, SAM and Grounding DINO + SAM.

Talker & Moses is the one classical method built for arbitrary viewpoints and non-upright books. It grows spine candidates with a shape-dependent active contour, filters line segments by structural constraints, and selects the final spines — as a **maximum weighted independent set on a conflict graph** in the WACV abstract, by rules over a tree of overlapping candidates in the thesis, which is the version we read ([Haifa CRIS record of the WACV 2014 paper, peer-reviewed](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/viewpoint-independent-book-spine-segmentation/); [2013 MSc thesis PDF, open](https://runi.ac.il/media/dwefkmbw/book-spine-segmentation-for-bookshelf-reorganization-2013.pdf)). That last step is the most directly portable idea for our failures of merged close lines and posts boxed as books. The thesis, read in full ([`talker-2013-spine-segmentation.md`](talker-2013-spine-segmentation.md)), reports P 80.7 / R 87.3 and P 70.4 / R 72.6 on its two sets, one parameter set tuned on the same images; its candidates alone reach recall 95 / 82 at precision ~38, so the selection is where its precision comes from.

The same failures recur across all three waves. Neighbours have low contrast. Titles and graphics draw false vertical lines. Books tilt. Perspective distorts the photo's edges. Identical adjacent books merge. Two-tone spines split in two. Non-book objects get boxed as books. The best deep detector on library shelves still **misaligns spine top boundaries**, misses five tilted copies of an identical book, and misses distorted spines at the photo's left edge ([Ma et al. 2024, *Sensors*, peer-reviewed, CC BY](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11679322/)). Our top/foot errors are therefore a general hard sub-problem, not a defect peculiar to our texture-change rule. SAM on library shelves segments text-bearing non-books such as section labels. It splits two-coloured spines, merges adjacent books and picks up partial books from neighbouring shelves ([Llabrés et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19812)). That is the same hazard our Felzenszwalb colour fallback faces, and it argues for a shape prior (elongated, spanning the row) before a colour region counts as a spine. No source studies dark plain spines, glare, shelf decorations or 90°/180°-turned photos specifically. Our worst failure classes are undocumented territory.

Several routes were tried and abandoned. Grounding DINO + SAM was dropped by its own hobbyist author for a fine-tuned detector because it "requires significant computational resources" ([capjamesg/open-shelves, self-published](https://github.com/capjamesg/open-shelves)). The Trixi robot abandoned text and SIFT matching for **HSV colour histograms**, because its small text was illegible in too few clear camera images, and SIFT confused look-alike books ([Wieczorek et al. 2022, arXiv preprint](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.10110)). Ma et al. list the limitations they attribute to predecessors. Horizontal-box YOLO "does not address elongated spines". Multi-angle rotation before detection is slow. Angle regression loses accuracy on upright spines because of angle periodicity near 90°, the most common angle. Dense rotated anchors are costly ([Ma et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11679322/)).

## Few numbers exist, and almost none are comparable to ours

The only rigorous spine-detection benchmark is Ma et al. It uses **661 smartphone photos of Shenzhen University library shelves** (283 tilted, 378 within 5° of vertical) and **15,454 polygon-labelled spines, about 23 per image**, close to our ~22 per photo. The metric is AP at a strict IoU of 0.75 on oriented boxes ([Ma et al. 2024, peer-reviewed](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11679322/)). Two readings of the same paper disagree, so the figures below need care. Its Table 4 spine-class AP@0.75 gives **YOLOv8n-OBB 75.19 at 26.7 FPS, 10.9 MB and 29.6 GFLOPs**, plain Oriented R-CNN 81.41, and the proposed model 90.20 at 45 MB and 195 GFLOPs. A two-class mAP that includes the easier call-number-label class is also quoted, with YOLOv8n-OBB at 81.32. The spine-class figure is the one that matches our task. The paper's own prose also contradicts its table for three baselines, and its FPS hardware is unclear. The largest single gain in its ablation came from **replacing square anchors with clustered elongated ones (height/width ratios 1.79, 7.05, 15.94)**, which raised spine AP from 71.25 to 81.41. Anchor-free YOLOv8 sidesteps that issue.

Llabrés et al. gives the only end-to-end numbers on a modern pipeline. It uses 285 photos from a Barcelona public library with 7,536 books, mostly in Catalan, Spanish and English plus some Arabic. Detection is SAM, OCR is Amazon Rekognition, and matching is CLIP. It reports no IoU precision or recall, only a manual mask review: **9,850 masks, 6,912 books, 1,267 non-books, 256 merged books** ([arXiv preprint, DAS 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19812)). Our research team derived a rough figure from those counts that the authors did not report: about 82% of text-bearing masks are books, and about 92% of books are found. That puts a commercial-OCR-gated foundation model in the **same band as our P 0.67 / R 0.89**, with no IoU criterion on either side. The comparison is directional, not a benchmark.

Identification numbers are older and partly garbled. Smart Library reports top-1 text-only P/R/F **0.92/0.90/0.91** on 454 Stanford spine images against about 9,000 books, with MRR 0.91. It quotes Tsai 2011's text-only result as 0.92/0.60/0.72 ([Yang et al. 2016, preprint](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1611.07385)). A university listing reports Tsai's hybrid at about 91% recall and each single pipeline at about 72% ([NTHU listing, secondary](https://scholars.nthu.edu.tw/esploro/outputs/conferencePaper/Combining-image-and-text-features-A/9957768442206774)). Smart Library's transcription of the hybrid's precision and recall is internally inconsistent with its F of 0.91. That stays unresolved until the Stanford PDF, which was blocked here, is read. A 2025 *Electronics* paper claims **96.8% single-book recognition** with YOLOv11 + PaddleOCR ([MDPI, peer-reviewed but known only from a search snippet; the page returned 403](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/14/23/4689)). Boatsure's **99.18% top-1** is measured in *simulated* scenes against a closed list ([GitHub, self-published](https://github.com/Boatsure/DL-On-shelf-Books-Recognition)). Neither number transfers to open home shelves.

The library field test is sobering. The UJI drone read call-number labels with Tesseract and reached **65% overall** recognition plus localisation on 88 books, 72% if unreadable tags are excluded. OCR accuracy fell from 75% to about 25% at low resolution, and the authors say a 35% error rate "implies considerable inventory loss" ([Martinez-Martin et al. 2021, *Sensors*, peer-reviewed](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7915268/)). The vision systems that work in libraries read **short, format-checkable call-number labels, not titles**. Libraries that need near-perfect inventory used RFID instead: A*STAR's AuRoSS claims "up to 99%", but that is a press figure with no breakdown ([A*STAR highlight, self-published](https://research.a-star.edu.sg/articles/highlights/high-tech-librarian-knows-its-books/)). Some even added machine-readable tags rather than read print, as ShelvAR did ([Popular Science 2011, press](https://www.popsci.com/technology/article/2011-04/augmented-reality-app-librarians-instantly-shows-which-books-are-misfiled/)). A home tool has neither tags nor labels, so it should expect the vision regime's ceiling and design for user correction. The commercial precedent, Shelfie/BitLit, identified "most books" but suffered from flash glare. It **shut down in January 2017 for business reasons, not accuracy** ([Publishers Weekly, press](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/digital/content-and-e-books/article/72650-shelfie-closes.html)).

## Retail shelves contribute the post-processing our merge step lacks

Dense retail detection solved a problem our spacing-relative line merging handles ad hoc: deciding which of many overlapping candidates are distinct objects. SKU-110K replaced greedy NMS with an **EM-Merger**. Each candidate becomes a Gaussian with covariance (h/4)², (w/4)², weighted by a predicted IoU score. K clusters are fitted by EM, with K set from image area over mean object area, initialised by agglomerative clustering. Each output box takes the median size of the boxes inside its 2σ ellipse. This was the biggest jump in their ablation: **AP .413 → .482 and precision at recall 0.5 (on the IoU 0.75 curve, [`goldman-2019-sku110k.md`](goldman-2019-sku110k.md)) .491 → .802**. The quality score alone, used with ordinary NMS, barely helped (.418) ([Goldman et al. 2019, CVPR, peer-reviewed](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.00853)). On a bookshelf row the problem is nearly one-dimensional. Candidate spine boundaries from Sobel lines, text-map gaps and colour regions become 1-D Gaussians along the row. K comes from row length over median spine width, a prior the row measures for itself. Even a crude score such as edge strength or text-map coverage could weight them. Our team's inference is that this costs a few dozen lines of numpy. It is untested on spines.

Retail also shows that **context pruning needs a recovery step**. Tonioni & Di Stefano match local features to product models. They then prune detections inconsistent with a neighbour graph, which raises precision from about 78% to about 98% but leaves recall at about 74%. A third step predicts each missing item's region from its accepted neighbours and verifies it, and that brings both to **about 90/90** on 70 shelf images ([Tonioni & Di Stefano 2017, preprint, ICIAP by common citation](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1707.08378)). On a bookshelf the graph collapses to left and right neighbours in a row. A box whose width, top or foot departs wildly from both neighbours is the signature of a post or frame. A gap wider than the row's spacing statistics is where a thin or dark spine was missed. Retail's own "banners and distractors" are the analogue of our posts.

Unitail annotates 1.78M retail products as **quadrilaterals**, because axis-aligned and rotated boxes overlap heavily on dense, tilted shelves and get suppressed ([Chen et al. 2022, ECCV, peer-reviewed](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.00298)). That supports our homography-quadrilateral representation, which generalises an oriented box. Shelf rectification gives only modest gains in retail. A fixed global homography adds **+2 to 5 mAP points**, and the gain shrinks or reverses above 40 objects per image ([Sah & Mathew 2026, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.08126v1)). That argues for per-row rotation and shear over a whole-image warp. Recurring-pattern detection for identical facings helps feature-rich packaging but costs about O(n³), and its weak case is texture-poor items (81%) ([Liu et al. 2016, preprint](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.06647)). It will not rescue dark plain spines.

## Reading should go per line, keep alternatives, and fold Hebrew look-alikes

Smart Library reads **per detected text line**. It uses a CNN upside-down classifier that flips when p > 0.7, keeps when p < 0.3 and **keeps both orientations in between**. Recognition is a CNN-BiLSTM-CTC with a character-level seq2seq corrector. On cropped scene words Tesseract scored **60.1 / 57.3 / 65.9%** on IC03-50, IC03-Full and SVT-50 against 98.2 / 95.8 / 94.6% for the authors' recogniser ([Yang et al. 2016, preprint](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1611.07385)). Those are scene words, not spines. No source reports a per-line character or word error rate for Tesseract on spines. Our whole-crop Tesseract configuration is the one with the weakest reported support, and our "every quarter-turn, keep the most confident" rule is a brute-force version of their classifier. Their 0.3–0.7 band suggests handing the matcher both readings rather than committing in the reader.

Spine direction is a prior, not a rule. ISO 6357:1985 (confirmed 2023) prefers a transverse title where it fits, otherwise a descending one, and calls ascending titles not standard. Its scope covers only Roman, Greek and Cyrillic scripts ([ISO catalogue](https://www.iso.org/standard/12665.html); [iTeh preview](https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/iso/fe15b4c9-b490-4e85-b6bc-27845c6c4382/iso-6357-1985)). Practice varies by region: American books mostly run descending, while British books historically and German books typically run ascending ([ABAA glossary](https://www.abaa.org/glossary/spine); [Dan Q blog, self-published](https://danq.me/2019/01/27/iso-6357/)). For Hebrew, ANSI/NISO Z39.41-1997 §6.2 says a descending title in a right-to-left script "should be reversed, i.e., right to left", and an edge title goes on the right edge ([`niso-z3941-spines.md`](niso-z3941-spines.md), read in full). If our reading of that clause holds, a Hebrew and an English spine both read top to bottom but want opposite quarter turns to stand upright. One forum typesetter likewise saw every Hebrew spine run top-to-bottom ([InDesign-talk thread, anecdote](https://groups.google.com/g/indesign-talk/c/rA4DdLNtpUY)). Both rotations must stay in play. Inside a descending Hebrew spine the letters still run right-to-left, so line rotation and text direction must be decided independently. ANSI/NISO Z39.41 reserves spine-foot area for library labels ([NISO project page](https://groups.niso.org/higherlogic/ws/public/projects/126/details)), so second-hand ex-library books may carry call-number text the reader should not take as the title. Z39.41's clauses are in [`niso-z3941-spines.md`](niso-z3941-spines.md).

Hebrew OCR evidence is thin and entirely self-published. Stock Tesseract `heb` scores **CER 0.226 / WER 0.473 on HeOCR's 38 printed pages**, and the best fine-tune lowers CER only to 0.209 ([HeOCR R6, Zenodo, self-published](https://zenodo.org/records/24109989)). The heb_rivok fine-tune (Apache-2.0) self-reports 4.7% → 1.4% error on Hebrew-only document lines but **19.3% → 14.0% on mixed Hebrew/English lines** ([GitHub RivoksLab, self-published](https://github.com/RivoksLab/tesseract-heb-rivok)). Mixed lines, which spines often carry, are several times harder. None of these covers scene text or spines. Practitioner guides name the confusions **ו/ז/ן, ה/ח/ת, ב/כ, מ/ס and final forms output as medial** ([kovetz.co.il](https://www.kovetz.co.il/en/guides/ocr-pdf-hebrew); [fastocr.org](https://fastocr.org/hebrew-ocr)). That matches our one-look-alike-letter corrector. Bar-Ilan work (an arXiv preprint with a related peer-reviewed journal version) finds that a light corrector trained on synthetic errors mimicking the **target corpus's own OCR error profile** beats random noise injection and spellcheckers on historical Hebrew newspapers ([Suissa et al. 2023, arXiv preprint; journal version in ACM JOCCH](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.16220)). The lesson for us is to weight corrections by a confusion matrix measured on our own spine crops rather than a generic list. Swapping Tesseract's `heb` for heb_rivok is a cheap, browser-compatible experiment, scored only on our labelled books.

Small VLMs are not a near-term route. The only spine evidence is a single-photo blog test in which every small model read a Bruce Lee spine as "Tao of Jeff Kune Do" ([daconta.us, self-published, n=1](https://daconta.us/Articles/slm-image-recognition-test-and-impressions.html)). That is fluent completion of a familiar title, the failure a matcher must reject. They are also out of reach in a no-GPU browser.

## Matching against a list rescues reading, and the list matters more than the matcher

Llabrés et al. ran matching on ground-truth segments against three lists: the exact titles in the photo, a 15k library inventory, and 2.3M GoodReads titles. String matching scores **0.783 / 0.742 / 0.505**. CLIP scores 0.939 / 0.915 / **0.449**, and CLIP + Hungarian one-to-one assignment scores 0.984 / 0.918 ([arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19812)). With detection, the 15k-list figures fall to 0.573 for string matching and 0.641 for CLIP + Hungarian. On the open 2.3M list, **plain fuzzy string matching degrades most gracefully and beats CLIP**. Hungarian assignment's gain shrinks to +0.003 as distractors grow. A closed or user-specific candidate list dominates the choice of matcher. The paper reports no "not in list" rejection rate, which is the practical problem for a home library. For our separate title matcher this means three things. It should receive n-best strings and both orientations. It should fold Hebrew final forms (ך→כ, ם→מ, ן→נ, ף→פ, ץ→צ) and matres lectionis (ו/י) before matching. It should enforce one-to-one assignment only against a closed list, such as the user's previously confirmed books. These are our team's inferences from one preprint. No Hebrew-catalogue matching result exists anywhere. Stanford's 2011 hybrid added an image-feature pipeline because "some book spines contain only text components that do not yield distinguishing image features" ([NTHU listing](https://scholars.nthu.edu.tw/esploro/outputs/conferencePaper/Combining-image-and-text-features-A/9957768442206774)). With no Hebrew spine-image database that route is closed for first identification. It could still re-identify a user's already-confirmed books across photos.

No public leaderboard exists, and Trixi's authors note there is **no standardized benchmark for book-spine recognition** ([Wieczorek et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.10110)). The table maps each dataset to the quantity it can score without fabricating labels.

Nothing public scores **Hebrew spine reading, home-shelf detection, or posts and frames as false positives**. Those stay on our own labelled photos, extended only by the owner annotating more. Synthetic Hebrew spine strips are fine for training a recogniser but must never serve as scoring ground truth. The Shenzhen set is the one free, honest detection transfer test. Its regime gap, with uniform library spines, call-number stickers and (most likely) Chinese titles, means a score there measures robustness, not our target.

For metrics, keep P/R/F at IoU 0.5, the classical retail and library convention ([Tonioni & Di Stefano 2017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1707.08378)). Add IoU 0.75 to match Ma et al. ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11679322/)), because a 0.5 hit on a 15-pixel spine can be off by a whole neighbour. Add per-image count MAE, SKU-110K's direct measure of merge and split errors ([Goldman et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.00853)). Compute IoU on our quadrilaterals. Following the UJI split ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7915268/)), report boxed-but-unreadable separately from never-boxed. Break results down by spine class: dark plain, thin, leaning, transverse versus rotated, Hebrew versus English versus mixed. End-to-end, report the fraction of books correctly matched. Adopt SKU-110K's split rule: **never put the same physical shelf in both tuning and held-out sets**. A held-out home or phone the pipeline never saw plays the role of Unitail's cross-domain g-mAP ([Chen et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.00298)).

## Concrete changes, ranked by evidence and cost

The first change is to **estimate and remove each row's dominant lean** before searching for lines. This is Smart Library's retained classical step, and it addresses our leaning-book and turned-photo failures together. The second is to **over-generate boundary candidates** from Sobel lines, text-map gaps and Felzenszwalb regions, the regions required to be elongated and to span the row. Choose among them either by a 1-D EM-Merger with K from the row's own spacing, or by a weighted independent set (Talker & Moses's WACV abstract; the thesis uses tree rules). Either replaces pairwise spacing-relative merging with one global choice. The third is a **row-neighbour consistency pass with recovery**. Prune boxes whose geometry departs from both neighbours, which targets posts and frames. Then re-examine gaps wider than the row's median spine width at a lower edge threshold, which targets thin and dark spines. Tonioni's 78→98→90 sequence warns that pruning alone costs recall.

On reading, the fourth change is to read per text line from the PP-OCRv4 detection map and stop running whole-crop Tesseract. Decide rotation and line direction separately, and emit **n-best strings for both plausible orientations** to the matcher. The fifth is to trial heb_rivok against stock `heb` on our labelled crops. Replace the generic look-alike list with a confusion matrix measured on our crops, and fold final forms and ו/י at the matching stage rather than word by word.

The one learned step with a measured reference point is a **YOLOv8n-OBB or YOLO11n-seg** trained on the CC0 Shenzhen set and exported to ONNX for onnxruntime-web. It would serve as an extra candidate source feeding the same global selection, not as a replacement, measured at spine AP@0.75 75.19 on library shelves ([Ma et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11679322/)). Two cautions apply. Ultralytics code is AGPL-3.0, and how that applies to exported weights was not verified. No CPU or WASM latency for any spine detector has been published. This should be gated behind the classical improvements above and adopted only if it measurably beats them on our own held-out photos.

## Conclusion

The literature changes where effort should go more than how. Our pipeline is not behind the classical state of the art. That art never measured itself, and its learned successors measured themselves on library shelves with call-number stickers. The real gap is structural. Our pipeline makes local, pairwise decisions (merge these two lines, veto this one, keep the most confident rotation) where the successful systems make **one global choice over too many candidates and keep alternatives alive until a list can arbitrate**. EM-Merger, the weighted independent set, neighbour recovery and n-best matching are all forms of that single idea, and all are cheap enough for Pyodide.

The second implication is about evidence. Every external number either measures a different regime or comes from a preprint or self-published source. So the most valuable investment is not another method. It is a scorer that reports IoU 0.5 and 0.75 on quadrilaterals, count error and per-spine-class breakdowns, plus a held-out shelf from a different home. Without those, a change to the 6-photo P 0.67 / R 0.89 cannot be told apart from noise, and no dataset in the world can do that scoring for us.
